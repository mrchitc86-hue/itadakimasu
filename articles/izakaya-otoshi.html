Otoshi, explained: why an izakaya charges you for a dish you didn't order

You sat down, asked for a beer, and a small bowl you didn't order arrived with it. Later it's on the bill — a few hundred yen, per person. It isn't a mistake and it isn't a scam. It's otoshi, the izakaya cover charge in the form of a small dish, and once you know what it's doing there, the evening makes sense. Here's the first five minutes of a Japanese izakaya, in order.

7 min read · Written 12 September 2026

A dish you didn't order just arrived. Here's what it is

You found an izakaya (居酒屋) — the Japanese pub — and ordered a beer. It came with a small bowl: some simmered vegetable, a little dressed squid, a spoonful of potato salad. Nobody asked if you wanted it, and at the end there's a line on the bill for it, once for every person at the table.

That bowl is otoshi (お通し). The short version: it's a seat charge that arrives as food. Everyone pays a fixed amount, whether they eat it or not, and it doesn't change with what you order afterwards. It isn't a mistake, a free welcome snack or a mark-up for foreigners — the Japanese group at the next table is paying exactly the same line.

In eastern Japan it's otoshi; in the west, around Osaka and Kyoto, it's tsukidashi (突き出し). Same thing. The word is often explained as coming from "passing" an order through to the kitchen, though there are other stories.

How much? A few hundred yen per person is the normal range; pricier places charge more. Per head, not per table, so four people pay it four times.

It's an izakaya and small-bar thing. Not ramen shops, set-meal places, beef-bowl chains, family restaurants, conveyor-belt sushi or cafés — and never anywhere with a ticket machine by the door, because there you paid before you sat down.

What is otoshi? The izakaya cover charge, served as food

Follow the money and it's simple: number of people × a fixed amount. Three dishes or one beer for two hours, the line is the same. On the bill it's お通し or お通し代 (otoshi-dai); some shops label the same line 席料 (seat charge) or チャージ.

Japan has no tipping and, outside hotels and expensive restaurants, no service charge. Otoshi is about the only way a casual place puts a price on the seat itself — a cover charge, handed over as a bowl of food.

Where is it written? Often at the bottom of the menu in small type, sometimes by the door, at the chains on the tablet's first screen. And sometimes nowhere — which is where most arguments start: some shops don't print it, and you find out on the bill. In tourist areas you'll sometimes see an English line explaining it.

Can you refuse it? It depends on the shop. Some will leave it off if you say so before it arrives; many treat it as the seat charge and won't. Both are the shop's policy, and neither is a trick. Some chains let you choose your otoshi, or skip it — worth asking.

From the kitchen side: what the small dish is actually doing

One of us is a chef who spent ten years in Japanese restaurant kitchens, so here is the view from the other side of the counter.

A dish like this does three jobs for the shop, and none of them is ripping you off.

It buys the kitchen time. A beer takes a minute to pour; the first skewers take ten or fifteen, and the whole table orders at once. The bowl gives you something to pick at while the kitchen gets through the rush.

It's made in advance. Otoshi is cooked before service in one batch, portioned into bowls and lined up in the fridge, so anyone on the floor can hand it over in seconds. That's why it's so often a cold simmered dish, a dressed vegetable, something marinated: those lose nothing by sitting a few hours, and some get better. It's also why otoshi is almost never hot and crisp.

It costs the shop very little, and it protects the seat. The bowl is usually made from trimmings, cheap cuts, whatever the prep left over, so the ingredients are a small part of what you pay. Most of the charge is for the chair, which is what lets a shop stay open with one customer on one beer for two hours.

One more thing the regulars know: the first bowl is the shop showing its hand. A place that doesn't care sends out something from a packet; a place that does puts thought into it. So if the otoshi is good, order more. You're in a good shop.

The first five minutes, in order

From the door to the first sip. It's the same in almost every izakaya.

  1. The door. Nan-mei sama? — how many? Hold up fingers. If you're shown to a raised tatami floor or a sunken table, shoes come off at the step.
  2. The towel. A rolled wet towel, oshibori (おしぼり), arrives. Free, and nothing to do with otoshi. Hands, not face.
  3. Drinks first. Staff come for drinks only; food can wait. The stock phrase is toriaezu nama (とりあえず生) — "a draught beer, to start".

    Nomihodai, briefly

    You'll be asked now if you want nomihodai (飲み放題), all-you-can-drink, sold by the time slot: typically 90 minutes or two hours, last orders 20–30 minutes before the end, often the whole table has to be on it, and usually a food course or a minimum order comes with it. For a couple of beers it isn't worth it.

  4. The dish arrives with the drinks. That's the otoshi. Water, ohiya (お冷や), is free if you ask.
  5. Kanpai. People generally wait until everyone has a glass, then raise them together.
  6. Then the food, in rounds. Order a few things, share from the middle, order more. To call: a button on the table, a tablet, or sumimasen! across the room — not rude here.

Beer, happoshu and "the third beer": why Japan has three kinds

At the izakaya, nama (生) is draught beer, and it's beer. But in a konbini or supermarket there's a wall of cans that all look like beer, at three prices. The small print says ビール (beer), 発泡酒 (happōshu) or リキュール（発泡性）① — liqueur, sparkling.

Why? For a long time Japan's liquor tax sorted beer-like drinks by how much malt they contained: more malt, more tax. So the brewers made happōshu, with less malt, and then the "third beer" — shin-janru, dai-san no bīru — with no malt at all, or another spirit blended in, to land in the cheapest bracket. Three tiers of price, made not by taste but by the tax table.

So read the label. 発泡酒 or リキュール means lighter and cheaper, on purpose; if you wanted beer, look for ビール. The same logic is part of why a lemon sour or a highball — shōchū or whisky with soda — is often cheaper than beer on the same menu.

The tax that built the three tiers is being flattened, in stages. Where that stands right now is below.

Right now: Japan's beer tax changes on 1 October (September 2026)

  • On 1 October 2026 the liquor tax on beer, happōshu and third beer becomes a single rate — the last step of a change phased in over several years (National Tax Agency). For you: the tax part of the three-tier price gap disappears. Whether shelf prices move, and by how much, is up to each maker and shop.
  • The direction: beer's tax goes down; happōshu and third beer's go up. So the cheapest cans lose their tax advantage. We won't guess what happens to a draught beer at your izakaya — that's the shop's decision.
  • Some makers have said they're rebuilding third-beer products as beer. Same brand name, different word on the can.

Paying, leaving, and when something on the bill looks wrong

The bill, denpyō (伝票), is usually in a holder on the table or clipped to the wall. Most often you take it to the till by the door; sometimes you pay at the table. Okaikei onegaishimasu (お会計お願いします) — the bill, please. No tip.

Cash. Small izakaya can still be cash only; cards, IC cards and QR depend on the shop, and some tills won't split one bill across several cards. Carry enough for the table; if you don't, the ATMs in most convenience stores take foreign cards.

  • There's a line you don't recognise. That's the otoshi, once per person.
  • You didn't want it. Before it arrives, otōshi wa iranai desu (お通しはいらないです) — "no otoshi, please" — and some shops will leave it off. If they say no, it's the seat charge there. Let it go; arguing costs more than the bowl.
  • You can't eat it (allergy, vegetarian, halal). Say so early and many shops will swap the bowl. Where they can't, the charge may stay — it's the seat you're paying for.
  • It wasn't written anywhere, and it's large. Rare. The rip-off stories you read about are usually this shape. If it's thousands of yen rather than hundreds, ask for the itemised bill and keep the receipt.
  • Your nomihodai time ran out. Last orders means last orders; some shops sell an extension — ask.
