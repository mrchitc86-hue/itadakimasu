Bento: what Japanese people really eat for lunch

Search "bento" in English and you get rice shaped into cartoon faces and boxes colour-matched like a paint chart. Those are real. But the box a Japanese office worker or student opens on a Tuesday is plainer than that, and much more practical. Here's what's in it, why it's eaten cold, and how it gets made in the twenty minutes before everyone leaves the house.

7 min read · Written September 2026 · By Kats, chef and licensed cook

Photo: a weekday lunchbox, open

What a bento is

A bento is one meal, packed to be carried. Rice and side dishes in a box, taken from home or bought from a shop. School, work, day trips, trains. Bringing your lunch isn't a thrifty eccentricity here — adults and children both do it.

Three broad kinds:

  • Made at home — what this article is about.
  • Bought — convenience stores, supermarkets, lunchbox shops. There's more on this in the convenience store article.
  • Ekiben (駅弁) — bought at a station for a train journey. Its own thing, for another article.

The shape of the box: half rice, half everything else

A home bento nearly always fits this pattern.

  • Rice takes a third to a half of the box. Plain white rice with a pickled plum, a shake of furikake seasoning, or a sheet of nori on top. Sometimes it's packed as small onigiri instead — easier to eat with your hands, and it won't collapse in transit.
  • One main: rolled omelette, fried chicken, grilled salmon, a hamburg steak, ginger pork.
  • Two or three sides: broccoli, kinpira (braised burdock and carrot), blanched spinach, cherry tomatoes.
  • Colour, deliberately: red (tomato, pickled plum), yellow (egg), green (broccoli). Get those three in and the box reads as "a proper bento".

Ours usually holds rolled omelette, a boiled egg, sausages and meatballs. All of them taste right cold, all of them are quick in the morning, and children and adults both eat them. Egg turns up so often that you could call it the real main of the Japanese lunchbox — which is why, when egg prices rise, people start talking about bento (see Right now, below). Incidentally, tamagoyaki is close to a different dish in every household. That's its own article.

Why it's eaten cold

This is the part visitors find strangest. A Japanese bento is built to be eaten at room temperature. Some offices have a microwave and some people use it; plenty don't, and it isn't a hardship.

That works because the cooking aims at cold from the start:

  • Rolled omelette is made slightly sweet, because sweetness carries when the egg is cold.
  • Fried chicken is marinated a little harder than you would for dinner.
  • Rice is cooled before packing, so the steam doesn't turn it damp in the box.
  • Anything wet is left out, or drained first.

So it isn't a hot meal that has gone cold. It's food whose finished state is cold.

Honestly: a weekday bento is mostly frozen food

Time to drop the pretence. Even in the house of someone who spent more than a decade in professional kitchens, the weekday box is mostly frozen bento food. Fried chicken, meatballs, croquettes — sometimes even the rolled omelette. One reason: nobody spends time on the morning. That's the reality in Japanese homes, and there's no shame in it.

Japan's freezer aisle has evolved around exactly this. Items marked shizen kaitō (自然解凍) go into the box frozen and are ready by lunchtime. The rest take a minute in the microwave. The taste is good enough that a professional cook is happy to eat it. If you've come away with the impression that Japanese lunchboxes are elaborate, half the credit belongs to frozen-food manufacturers.

Which makes the actual morning look like this:

  1. Rice into the box, then leave it to cool — the longest step. Spreading it on a flat plate speeds it up. Or shape it into small onigiri.
  2. Two or three frozen items, microwaved or left to thaw on their own.
  3. One egg dish, rolled or boiled. This is usually the thing that's actually cooked.
  4. Cherry tomatoes or broccoli to fill the gaps.
  5. Lid on only once everything is cold. Close it warm and steam collects, which is both soggy and unsafe.

Ten to fifteen minutes. Not more. Some days are more handmade than that — but those are the days with time in them, not the ordinary ones.

Character bento are a separate matter. Kyaraben, where rice is shaped into an animal or a cartoon face for a child's school trip or sports day, genuinely exists. But it belongs to occasions, not to Tuesdays. The beautiful boxes you find when you search "bento" in English are nearly always that kind — a different purpose, and a different amount of time.

Keeping it safe: the summer box

Japanese summers are hot and humid, and a bento made at seven is eaten at noon without refrigeration. The household habits around that:

  • Keep water out — no wet dishes, drain the vegetables.
  • A pickled plum in the rice. It's long been said to help preserve it; the real effect is modest, but the habit has stayed.
  • An ice pack and an insulated bag.
  • No raw vegetables or raw fish in summer.
  • Leftovers from last night get reheated properly in the morning, not just packed.

Treat all of that as general practice rather than a rule — it depends on where you are, the season, and how the box is carried.

The box itself

  • Two-tier plastic — the most common. Rice on top, sides below.
  • Magewappa (曲げわっぱ) — bent cedar or cypress. The wood regulates moisture, so the rice tastes better cold. Expensive, and quietly coveted.
  • Insulated boxes — keep soup or rice warm. Popular in winter.
  • Sizes — roughly 800–900 ml for an adult man, 500–600 ml for a woman, 300–450 ml for a child. Shop shelves are labelled along those lines.

Made versus bought

A convenience store bento runs a few hundred yen; supermarket ones get discount stickers in the evening. Making it at home costs less than half that in ingredients, and twenty minutes of your morning. Most people here mix the two across a week. "Homemade every day" isn't the standard, and neither is any judgement about buying one.

Right now: what's changing around the lunchbox (September 2026)

  • Bought bento have got dearer. Kiyoken's famous shūmai bento went from ¥1,070 to ¥1,180 in February 2026, a second rise in two years, and became the symbol of the station lunchbox passing ¥1,000.
  • Convenience stores are going bigger instead. 7-Eleven's "big onigiri" line is the same idea: more food rather than a lower price (see the onigiri article).
  • Eggs are expensive. Both the rolled omelette and the boiled egg are lunchbox staples, and egg prices hit a record in the winter of 2025 and were still around 20 per cent above normal in August 2026. Our box leans on eggs, so this one isn't abstract.
  • Rice has come down. Supermarket rice has been falling since early September, which takes a little pressure off the made-at-home side.

Reported by Nikkei (February 2026), egg price coverage (August 2026) and Teikoku Databank (September 2026). Prices vary by region and change often; the shelf label is always right.
