Mentsuyu: the one bottle that runs a Japanese kitchen

Open the fridge in almost any Japanese home and there's a tall bottle of dark liquid next to the soy sauce. That's mentsuyu — and it seasons far more than noodles. Here's how to use mentsuyu: what "2x" and "3x" on the label actually mean, how much water to add for dipping, soup and simmering, and — the part nobody explains — why those numbers are different. I hold Japan's national cook's licence and have worked in a professional kitchen; at home, this bottle does most of the work.

7 min read · Written 12 September 2026 · By Kats, chef and licensed cook

The short version "2x" means 1 part mentsuyu + 1 part water gives you dipping-sauce strength. "3x" means 1 part + 2 parts water. Soup for hot noodles is thinner — as a common example, around 1:3–4 for a 2x bottle and 1:5–6 for a 3x. Simmered dishes start thinner still. Your bottle's label overrides everything in this article. The rest explains why, so you can cook without the label.

What's in the bottle, and why one bottle does so much work

Mentsuyu (麺つゆ) is soy sauce, sweetness (mirin or sugar) and dashi — stock from bonito flakes, kelp and the like — mixed and balanced in advance. That's all. But "in advance" is the whole point.

Most Japanese seasoning comes down to those three: soy, sweet, dashi. A soba shop makes kaeshi (かえし) — soy sauce and sweetener blended and rested — and dilutes it with dashi for each dish. Home mentsuyu is that kaeshi plus dashi, in a bottle. So what a household is really buying isn't ingredients. It's a ratio. That's why the same bottle turns up in simmered dishes, rice bowls and omelettes.

Its shelf-mates, briefly:

  • Soy sauce (醤油) — salt and aroma. No sweetness, no dashi.
  • Mentsuyu / tsuyu — the same thing, whatever extra words the brand name adds.
  • Shiro-dashi (白だし) — pale, dashi-forward, less sweet. For dishes where you don't want colour. Not a stand-in; more below.
  • Ponzu (ポン酢) — citrus sourness is the point. A different thing.

The yaki-onigiri in our onigiri article — brushed with soy sauce and grilled — often gets mentsuyu at home instead; the sugar browns faster, so keep the heat low. And the tamagoyaki in the breakfast article, "seasoned with stock"? On a weekday, that stock is often this bottle.

Reading the label: what "2x" and "3x" actually mean

The number is measured against dipping strength, not soup

The "1x" that "2x" is measured against is straight — dipping strength, not the broth you pour over hot noodles. So if you reason "it's 2x, I'll dilute it 1:1 and that's my soup", you get a salty bowl of udon. Most first-bottle failures are this one.

Bottles come as straight, 2x, 3x and 4x. The higher the number, the more meals per bottle and the less fridge space, which is one reason 3x is so common on Japanese shelves. The catch: small measuring errors matter more. (What's happening to the price is in the Right now section at the end.) You may not see 4x outside Japan.

One more thing on the back: set the nutrition panel next to the one on a soy-sauce bottle and mentsuyu is the lower in salt; the difference is the sweetness and dashi.

A worked example: one 3x bottle, three dishes

Same 3x bottle. Common examples; the bottle wins.

  • Dipping sauce: 50 ml mentsuyu + 100 ml water. One small cup, one person.
  • Hot noodle soup: 50 ml + 250–300 ml water. About one bowl.
  • Simmered dish: 50 ml + 300–400 ml water to start; decide in the last few minutes.

Why is the soup so much thinner? That's the next section, and the part worth remembering.

Why the dilution ratio changes with the dish

Three reasons: how much you swallow, how long it touches the food, and how hot it is.

How much of the liquid you actually swallow

With dipping sauce, only the thin coat on the noodles reaches your mouth, so it can be strong. With hot noodle soup you drink the liquid, so it has to be much weaker. In a simmered dish, the liquid soaks into the food and the rest reduces.

Cooks in Japan are generally taught to aim for a little under one per cent salt in a soup — close to the salinity of the body. Makers set their hot-soup ratio to land there, which is why different bottles end up as roughly the same soup. The number on the front is the bottle's business; the landing point is your mouth's.

How long it touches the food

Cold noodles touch the sauce for a second, and they bring water with them, so the sauce weakens with every dip. Hence: start strong, pour a little, top up.

A simmered dish takes time. The liquid reduces and gets stronger; meanwhile daikon and mushrooms release water, and potatoes drink it up. One of the first things a professional kitchen teaches: salt goes in and doesn't come out. So a simmered dish starts thinner than soup and gets decided in the last few minutes. Taste at the end, not the beginning.

Hot or cold

Cold dulls sweetness and flattens aroma, so a cold dipping sauce has to be strong or it tastes like nothing. Heat lifts the aroma, so a hot soup can be thin.

A summer dipping sauce that tastes weak is usually ice, wet noodles or over-chilling; fixes below.

Hold on to those three — amount, time, temperature — and you don't need to memorise the table, only why it looks the way it does.

What mentsuyu can stand in for — and what it can't

One principle. Mentsuyu carries salt (soy sauce), sweetness and dashi as a bundle. In a dish that wants all three, it stands in for soy sauce plus mirin plus dashi. In a dish that wants only one, the other two tag along uninvited. That's the whole rule; the rest is examples.

In a professional kitchen you'd keep the three apart and decide the ratio for each dish. At home the bottle decides for you — which is fine exactly as often as the dish wants that ratio, and weekday cooking wants it surprisingly often.

If you need a tsuyu without fish, or soy sauce without wheat: read the label. That's all I'll say here.

When it goes wrong: the four usual causes

  • Too salty. Usually a simmered dish made at the soup ratio that then reduced, or a 3x diluted as if it were a 2x. Fix: stretch it with dashi, not water (water thins the flavour along with the salt), or add vegetables to soak some up.
  • Everything tastes the same. The famous "mentsuyu taste": the ratio is identical every time, so different dishes wear the same face. Fix: finish with something that isn't in the bottle — acid (citrus, vinegar), something fresh (ginger, shiso, myōga), sesame oil, or a few drops of plain soy sauce. Too sweet? Add soy sauce, about a tenth of the mentsuyu.
  • Tastes thin, even at the right ratio. You diluted the dashi along with the salt, and at soup strength there isn't much of it left. Fix: swap some water for dashi — granules are fine — or drop in a pinch of bonito flakes and warm it through (oi-gatsuo). Granules aren't cheating; pick by the job.
  • Watery dipping sauce by the third bite. Noodles not drained, ice in the cup, or a cup that's too big. Fix: shake the noodles dry; a little sauce in a small cup, topped up. If you want ice, dilute less to allow for it.

Storage: once opened, it lives in the fridge. Concentrate keeps longer than straight, but not forever; the number of days on the bottle is the one to use.

Right now: mentsuyu prices and where to find it (September 2026)

  • It's getting dearer in Japan. Teikoku Databank counted 4,923 food items going up in price in September 2026, the most of any month this year; seasonings were the biggest group at 1,959, soy sauce and tsuyu among them. Imported bottles abroad often follow later, and a higher concentration means more meals per bottle, so a rise lands more lightly.
  • Where to find it outside Japan. Japanese and East Asian grocers, the world-foods aisle of a big supermarket, and online. Expect one concentration, not a choice, and quite possibly a Japanese-only label — which is what the table above is for.
  • The season. September is when Japanese shelves move from cold noodles to hot ones; the bottle comes out again at New Year for toshikoshi soba.

Reported by Teikoku Databank, September 2026. Prices vary by country and shop and change often; the label on the bottle is always right.
