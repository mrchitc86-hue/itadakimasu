The ticket machine, explained: how to order in Japan without speaking a word

Walk into a ramen shop or a set-meal chain and nobody comes to take your order. There's a machine by the door instead. Buy a ticket, hand it over, sit down — the whole transaction happens before you've said a word. Here's how to use a ticket machine at a Japanese restaurant, and why the steps are the same on every machine in the country.

7 min read · Written 10 September 2026

Photo: the machine by the door

Why so many restaurants make you pay first

A shokken (食券) is a paper ticket you buy from a machine before you sit down. It's your order and your receipt in one. The machine that sells it is a kenbaiki (券売機). Visitors often call it a vending machine, and it looks like one, but what it sells is the ticket, not the food. Once the ticket is in your hand, you've ordered and you've paid, and nobody needs to come to your table for either.

The reason is plain economics. These are cheap, fast places — a ramen counter, a set-meal chain, a stand-up noodle shop by the station — and most of them are run by one or two people. Nobody has time to take orders, bring bills, or count change at the table. There's no tipping to work out. Pay, eat, leave is the fastest order there is, so that's the order.

It's also the reverse of what most visitors are used to, which is why the first time feels slightly wrong. You walk in, nobody greets you with a menu, and you're not sure whether you're allowed to sit. You are. If you have a ticket, the hard part is over.

Where you'll meet one, roughly in the order a visitor does: set-meal and beef-bowl chains, ramen shops, stand-up soba and udon counters, some curry shops, the canteens inside train stations, and university or office cafeterias. Yayoiken (やよい軒), the set-meal chain from our breakfast article, works this way. Matsuya (松屋) is the useful warning: some of its branches have a ticket machine by the door, and others take your order at the seat and charge you on the way out. Same chain, same menu, different system. So the rule is simple: look just inside the door before you do anything else.

How to spot one, and what to check before you press anything

It stands just inside the door, before the seats. There are two kinds. The button type is a wall of small rectangular buttons, each with a dish name and often a picture, and the ones you can buy are lit. The touchscreen type is one big screen. Nearly all have the same parts: a slot for notes, a slot for coins, a slot where the ticket comes out, and a tray for change.

How do you know a shop uses one? Nobody comes to take your order. The machine by the door is lit up. There may be a sign with 食券 on it, or a line such as 先に食券をお買い求めください ("please buy a ticket first"). And if you're still standing there looking unsure, a member of staff will point at the machine. That's the entire conversation.

Language. Touchscreens often have a language button — a globe icon, or the words English / 中文 / 한국어 — usually in a corner of the screen. Button machines usually don't have one, but the buttons carry a picture or a dish name, and you can press a picture.

Money. This is the part that actually matters. Coins and ¥1,000 notes work in almost every machine. ¥5,000 and ¥10,000 notes are the gamble: some machines take them, some don't. Transit cards like Suica and QR-code payments are accepted by a growing number of machines, and credit cards vary from shop to shop. So: walk in with one ¥1,000 note. If you don't have one, the convenience store next door will fix it — buy a bottle of tea to break a larger note, or use the ATM, which takes foreign cards. How to use the machine: four steps (the machine changes, the steps don't)

Every machine looks a little different. Every machine works the same way.

Step by step

  1. Put your money in first. On a button machine, the buttons often don't light up until there's money in — you can't press anything yet. Some touchscreens do it the other way round: choose first, pay at the end. Either way, do what the screen is asking for. The machine only lets you do one thing at a time, so it's hard to lose your place.
  2. Press the dish. Lit means you can buy it. Dark, or marked 売切, means sold out. The shop's main dish is often in the most obvious spot — the top row, or top left.
  3. Add size or extras, if you want. Large rice, an egg, miso soup, a topping — these are separate buttons, pressed after the main dish. Press nothing and you get the regular version. You're not missing anything.
  4. Take the ticket — and your change. The ticket drops into the slot at the bottom (if you bought two things, look for two tickets). Change is where people slip up: on some machines it falls into the tray on its own; on others there's a lever or a button marked おつり that you have to push. If nothing comes out, look for it. This is the step people forget.

What a button-type machine looks like

Here's the board as a drawing. Each bracket is a button; the notes on the right are what happens where.

 ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────┐
 │  [ notes in ]     [ coins in ]               │  ← 1. money goes in here
 ├──────────────────────────────────────────────┤
 │ [Beef bowl] [Beef bowl L] [Curry]  [Set A]   │  ← 2. lit = you can buy it
 │ [Miso soup] [Egg]  [Large rice]  [Salad]     │  ← 3. extras, all optional
 │ [sold out]  [Beer]  [Tea]  [Kids]            │     dark or 売切 = sold out
 ├──────────────────────────────────────────────┤
 │  ticket comes out here v        change tray  │  ← 4. take both (おつり = change)
 └──────────────────────────────────────────────┘

Top: money. Middle: dishes and extras. Bottom: ticket and change. On most machines your eyes travel down the board in that order, and that's the order you do things.

If it's a touchscreen

[Language] → [Category] → [Dish] → [Size / extras] → [Confirm] → [Pay] → ticket. On some machines [Pay] comes first — see step 1.

The only difference from the button machine is the order. The job is the same.

After the ticket: what happens at the counter

What you do with the ticket depends on the shop, and there are three patterns. Most often, you hand it to the staff at the counter. In some shops you put it on the counter or on a small tray in front of your seat and someone collects it. In others the ticket has a number on it, and your number is called or shown on a screen. If you can't tell which, sit down and hold the ticket where it can be seen. Someone will come for it.

Water, tea, chopsticks and condiments are often self-service: a jug on the counter or a dispenser against the wall, and a row of condiments. Take what you need. You don't have to ask.

Yayoiken is known for free rice refills — the breakfast article mentions it, and it's the thing that surprises most visitors. Policies change, so check the sign in the shop.

When you're done, there's no bill. You settled up at the door. If there's a return counter (返却口), take your tray there; if not, leave it. Some people say gochisōsama deshita — "thank you for the meal" — on their way out. Then you walk out, and nobody stops you.

When something goes wrong

  • You pressed the wrong button. Take the ticket to the staff, show it, and point at the right thing on the wall menu. If they haven't started cooking, they'll usually swap it.
  • The machine won't take your note. It's probably a ¥5,000 or ¥10,000 note. Say sumimasen, show the note, and the staff will often change it for you — the word is ryōgae. If not, the konbini.
  • No English, no pictures. Point at the photo on the wall menu. Staff will press the button for you.
  • The button is dark. Sold out. Pick something else.
  • Nothing came out. Either there's a final "print ticket" button you haven't pressed, or the ticket is sitting inside the slot. Bend down and look.
  • Card declined. Cash is the sure thing. Back to that one ¥1,000 note.

Do this once and you're set. A ramen shop, a stand-up soba counter, a curry place — the moves are the same: in the door, the machine, the ticket, a seat.
