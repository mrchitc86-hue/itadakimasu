Why Japan's convenience stores are selling clothes now

This month, 7-Eleven Japan put a fashion line on its shelves, FamilyMart's clothing brand turned five and announced pet goods, and the national news ran the story for a week. Here's what happened, and what it says about how people shop here.

5 min read · Written 10 September 2026 · Facts from company releases and Japanese press, linked at the end

Photo: socks and T-shirts on a konbini shelf

What happened this week

On 8 September, 7-Eleven Japan and adastria — the company behind fashion brands niko and ... and LOWRYS FARM — announced a 23-item clothing range for convenience stores. It goes on sale nationwide on 25 September: T-shirts, socks and eco-bags as the regular line, sweatshirts, scarves and mittens for the season, and a Hello Kitty collaboration arriving on 23 October. Prices run from ¥880 to ¥5,390. The stated target is women in their 20s to 40s — "busy, but not willing to compromise."

The same week, FamilyMart marked five years of its own clothing brand, Convenience Wear. The numbers behind it are the reason everyone else is now paying attention: the line passed ¥20 billion in annual sales in fiscal 2025, it's stocked in about 16,500 stores, and it has been quietly expanding from socks and underwear into outerwear, Imabari towels, and — from February 2027 — pet supplies.

Wait, convenience stores sold clothes before?

Yes, but not like this. For decades every konbini carried an emergency rack: plain socks, a white shirt for the salaryman who spilled coffee, underwear for the person who missed the last train. Nobody chose to shop for it. It was there for when you had no choice.

FamilyMart changed the framing in 2021. It hired designer Hiroaki Ochiai (of the Tokyo label FACETASM), gave the line a name and a look, and priced it to be bought on purpose: ¥429 socks in colour-coded stripes, ¥880 boxer shorts, ¥1,998 long-sleeve tees. The striped socks became a genuine hit — the kind of thing people post, collect, and give as small gifts. This summer the brand did a collaboration with Fuji Rock Festival, then one with a retired sumo grand champion whose socks sold out.

The shift, in one phrase Adastria's release describes the goal as moving konbini from "emergency purchase" to "purpose purchase" (緊急購買 → 目的購買). That is the whole story. A convenience store used to be where you bought socks because you had to. Now they'd like it to be where you buy socks because you want to.

Why now

Japanese press coverage this week — NHK, Yomiuri, Kyodo and others — pointed at the same few pressures:

  • Prices are up, visits are flat. Food inflation has pushed up the value of each basket, but the number of customers walking in has stalled. Chains need a new reason for people to come through the door.
  • Small supermarkets are eating their lunch. Compact urban supermarkets now sell fresh food at konbini convenience and lower prices. Groceries alone no longer set a konbini apart.
  • Younger customers, especially women, were drifting away. Both the 7-Eleven and FamilyMart launches name young women as the target. Clothes are a way to make a store feel current rather than merely open.

There's also a quieter logic. Socks and T-shirts don't expire. They don't need refrigeration. They sit on a shelf for months and still sell. For a business built on food that has to be thrown out at the end of the day, that is a very attractive product.

Photo: the striped Convenience Wear socks

What a konbini might look like in five years

FamilyMart has already built one. In July it opened FAMIMA PARK AZABUDAI in Tokyo — its first flagship, at 217 square metres roughly double a normal store. It has a Convenience Wear corner with a fitting room and dedicated staff, a delicatessen counter, coffee supervised by a champion barista, a rooftop garden, and multilingual self-checkout. FamilyMart's own phrase for it: a konbini you'd go out of your way to visit.

Most stores won't turn into that. But the direction is clear. The Japanese convenience store is being redesigned from a place you stop at into a place you go to — and the food, which is still what it does best, is coming along for the ride.

What this means if you're visiting

  • Yes, you can genuinely buy decent socks, tees and underwear at a konbini now. FamilyMart's are the established line; 7-Eleven's arrive from late September 2026.
  • The striped FamilyMart socks are a cheap, light, easy souvenir that people back home have probably never seen.
  • If you're in Tokyo and curious, FAMIMA PARK AZABUDAI (Toranomon 5-2-10, Minato-ku) is a look at where the format is heading.
