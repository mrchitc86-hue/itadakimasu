Onigiri: the rice ball that runs Japan

It's the food people pack for a hike, hand to a child, grab at 7 a.m., and eat at midnight. It's also the first thing most visitors buy and the first thing they get slightly wrong. Here's the whole story.

7 min read · Checked September 2026

Photo: three onigiri, one cut open

What it is

An onigiri (おにぎり) is a ball or triangle of plain cooked rice, lightly salted, usually with a filling in the middle and a strip of dried seaweed (nori) wrapped around the outside. That's all. It's not sushi — the rice isn't vinegared and there's no raw fish. It's closer in spirit to a sandwich: a way to make rice portable and eat it with your hands.

At home it's shaped by hand, palms damp and salted, which is where the name comes from — nigiru means "to grip." In shops it's shaped by machine, but the idea is the same.

The fillings to try first

Every convenience store carries a dozen or more. These are the ones almost everyone grows up on:

  • Salmon (鮭 / sake) — flakes of salted grilled salmon. The safe, beloved choice.
  • Tuna mayo (ツナマヨ) — canned tuna with mayonnaise. Invented for convenience stores, now a national favourite, especially with kids.
  • Pickled plum (梅 / ume) — a whole or chopped umeboshi, salty and very sour. The classic. Strong the first time; people who like it, love it.
  • Kombu (昆布) — kelp simmered in soy sauce until sweet and soft. Deep, savoury, no fish.
  • Okaka (おかか) — dried bonito flakes mixed with soy sauce. Smoky and light.
  • Mentaiko (明太子) — spicy salted cod roe. Rich, salty, a little hot. A grown-up favourite.

If you want one with no fish at all, kombu is the one. If you want to understand what Japanese people mean when they say "comfort food," it's salmon or ume.

An honest opinion from the author All the fillings are fun to try. But the one I keep coming back to is the plainest: rice, a little salt, a sheet of nori, nothing inside. It's called shio-musubi (塩むすび), and to me it's still the best of them all. When the rice is good, you don't need anything else.
How to open a konbini onigiri The wrapper keeps the seaweed dry and separate from the rice until you're ready to eat, so the nori stays crisp. There are numbered tabs. Pull tab 1 straight down the middle, all the way around. Then pull tabs 2 and 3 outward from each side — the plastic slides off and the seaweed wraps itself around the rice. Don't unwrap it like a present. It takes one try to get it wrong and one more to get it right.

Shapes and styles

  • Triangle. The standard shop shape. Easy to hold, easy to stack.
  • Round or barrel. More common at home and in regional styles.
  • Yaki-onigiri (焼きおにぎり) — no filling, brushed with soy sauce and grilled until the outside is crisp. Sold frozen and in izakaya pubs.
  • Tenmusu (天むす) — a small onigiri with a shrimp tempura sticking out of the top. A Nagoya speciality.
  • Omusubi. Another word for the same thing. Some people use it for the round ones; some shops use it because it sounds gentler. There's no rule.
What's new · September 2026 Onigiri just got cheaper, bigger, and — sometimes — naked

Rice prices are falling, all three konbini chains are cutting onigiri prices this month, and a poor nori harvest is changing what's on the outside.

Now in Japan: three things changing onigiri this autumn

1. Prices are coming down

After a year in which rice cost more than most people here had ever seen, the price has turned. Supermarket rice fell for three straight weeks into early September, and the new harvest is arriving cheaper than last year's stock — a reversal nobody remembers happening before. Convenience stores moved fast: 7-Eleven cut two of its best-sellers (grilled salmon, spicy cod roe) by ¥19 from 8 September, FamilyMart is taking ¥11 off four of its wrapped onigiri from 22 September, and Lawson cuts all twenty of its hand-rolled onigiri by ¥10 from 29 September. All three said the same thing: rice got cheaper, so did this.

2. They're getting bigger

The other trend is size. 7-Eleven announced an "Ookina Omusubi" line in early September with about 1.6 times the usual rice, in karaage and shrimp-mayo versions, and the Japanese press is calling it an autumn "giant onigiri war" between the chains. Meanwhile, at the cheap end, Lawson Store 100 is launching a ¥120 hand-rolled onigiri with nori from 16 September. Onigiri is being pulled in both directions at once: more for the money, and less money.

3. The seaweed problem

Nori — the sheet of seaweed on the outside — has had a rough few years. Warmer seas have hurt the harvest, and the wholesale price has risen to roughly 1.8 times what it was a few years ago. Makers are responding by switching to different growing regions or, in some products, leaving the nori off entirely. If you notice more onigiri sold "naked," or with a thinner strip than you expected, that's why. It also makes the plain shio-musubi with a full sheet of good nori feel a little more like a luxury than it used to.

Where the good ones are

Convenience stores are fine, and for most people that's where onigiri lives. But there's a step up:

  • Specialist onigiri shops. Small counters, often near stations, that make each one to order with rice cooked that morning. The seaweed is soft rather than crisp, the rice is loosely pressed, and the fillings are generous. Prices are higher than a konbini but still cheap for a meal.
  • Department-store food halls. The basement floor (depachika) of a big department store usually has an onigiri counter with unusual fillings.
  • Supermarkets. The prepared-food section has onigiri too, often marked down in the evening.

How to eat it

With your hands. That's the point. Bite straight in — there's no need to cut it, and no need to eat the seaweed separately. If it comes with a small packet of pickles or soy sauce, that's a bonus, not a requirement. Onigiri is eaten at room temperature or slightly cool; it's not usually heated.

One thing to know: rice balls are meant to be eaten the day they're made. Refrigeration makes the rice hard. If you buy one for later, keep it at room temperature and eat it within the "best before" time on the label.
