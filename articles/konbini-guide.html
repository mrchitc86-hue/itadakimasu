Konbini

The Japanese convenience store, explained

A konbini is not a corner shop with better lighting. It's where a large part of the country eats lunch, pays bills, and picks up dinner on the way home. Here's how to read one.

8 min read · Checked September 2026

Photo: konbini chilled shelf, evening

First, the basics

Almost every convenience store in Japan is one of three chains: 7-Eleven, FamilyMart, or Lawson. They're open 24 hours in most places, they're everywhere — often two on the same block — and they're clean, quiet and well-lit at 3 a.m. People call them konbini (コンビニ).

The thing visitors don't expect is the food. It's fresh, it's restocked several times a day, and a lot of it is genuinely good. Office workers eat konbini lunch by choice, not as a last resort.

A walk down the aisles

The chilled shelf: bento, rice balls, sandwiches

This is the heart of the shop. Boxed lunches (bento) with rice and a main dish, rice balls (onigiri) in their triangular wrappers, and crustless sandwiches in plastic triangles. The famous one is the egg sandwich — tamago sando — soft white bread filled with a creamy egg salad. It costs a few hundred yen and people genuinely travel to Japan and put it on their list.

The hot counter by the register

Next to the till there's a heated glass case. Inside, depending on the chain: fried chicken (FamilyMart's Famichiki and Lawson's Karaage-kun both have devoted fans), pork cutlet sticks, croquettes, and steamed buns filled with pork or sweet bean paste. You point, the staff bag it, and it goes on your bill.

From autumn through winter, this counter also holds a big steaming pot of oden (おでん) — a light broth with things simmering in it: boiled eggs, daikon radish, fish cakes, tofu, and konnyaku. You choose the pieces you want and they're ladled into a cup with broth. It's cheap, warm, and completely ordinary here, and it confuses almost every visitor the first time.

Drinks

A whole wall of them. Bottled tea (unsweetened green tea, barley tea, oolong) is the default drink here, not soda. There's also a hot-drink section near the register in winter — canned coffee and bottled tea kept warm in a heated case. If you want your coffee hot, that's where it is.

Most konbini also have a machine that grinds and brews fresh coffee. You buy the cup at the register, then fill it yourself. It's inexpensive and better than it has any right to be.

Bread and sweets

Soft, sweet buns and filled breads: melon bread (named for its crackly top, not its flavour), red bean buns, cream-filled rolls. The dessert fridge has cream puffs, pudding cups, and roll cakes that people hand out as small gifts.

Frozen food and instant noodles

The frozen section has proper meals — fried rice, gyoza dumplings, ramen — that people keep at home for weeknights. The instant noodle aisle is enormous. Every konbini has a hot-water dispenser next to the register so you can make a cup right there.

Photo: oden pot at the counter

How to use one without stress

  1. Take a basket. Even for two things. It's the norm.
  2. Bring things to the register. Point at anything in the hot case; the staff will get it for you.
  3. Expect one question. "Do you need a bag?" — bags cost a few yen, so most people say no and carry things in their hand or their own bag.
  4. Pay however you like. Cash, credit cards, transit cards like Suica, and phone payments all work. If you're paying cash, put it in the small tray rather than into the cashier's hand.
  5. Eat somewhere else. Many konbini have a small counter for eating in, but eating while walking down the street is considered a bit rough. A bench or park is fine.

Things that are only in konbini

  • Seasonal limited items. New flavours appear every few weeks and disappear just as fast. Sakura-flavoured everything in spring, sweet potato and chestnut in autumn.
  • An ATM that takes foreign cards. For visitors this may be the most useful thing in the shop. The ATMs in 7-Eleven (run by Seven Bank) and in most Lawson and FamilyMart branches accept overseas debit and credit cards, have an English menu, and work around the clock. When you need cash in Japan, a konbini is the answer.
  • Bill payment and tickets. The multi-purpose machine near the door prints concert tickets and lets you pay utility bills. Not food, but part of why people are always in there.
  • A clean toilet. Usually free to use, usually spotless. Ask first at the register if there's no sign.
