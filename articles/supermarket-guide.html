The Japanese supermarket: what to buy, and when the stickers come out

The convenience store is for convenience. The supermarket is for price and quantity. If you're in Japan for more than a few days, or you want to understand what Japanese households actually eat, this is the building to walk into. Everything a family buys, what it costs and when they come to get it is laid out on these shelves. Here's how to read it — including the discount-sticker timings nobody tells you about.

8 min read · Written September 2026

Photo: the prepared-food counter in the early evening

How it differs from a convenience store

  • Price. The same onigiri or drink is cheaper here.
  • Quantity. Vegetables, meat and fish only exist here — a konbini stocks little more than a bag of cut salad.
  • Hours. Convenience stores run around the clock. Supermarkets have shortened since the pandemic: 10 a.m. to 8 p.m. is now the common pattern.
  • The mood. You stop at a konbini. You go shopping at a supermarket.

Read this as the sequel to the convenience store article: that one is the shop for when you're stuck, this is the shop people live out of.

Door to till: how a Japanese supermarket is laid out

They're built to roughly the same plan.

  1. Vegetables and fruit, immediately inside. Whatever's in season is piled at the front. Every label carries the growing region.
  2. Fish. Fillets, sashimi, whole fish. Packs are marked for eating raw or for cooking.
  3. Meat, mostly thin-sliced for hotpot or stir-frying. Few thick steaks; prices are per 100 g.
  4. Prepared food (惣菜) — fried things, lunchboxes, sushi, salads. The busiest corner of the shop in the early evening.
  5. Tofu, natto, pickles, eggs. The contents of a Japanese fridge, on a shelf.
  6. Dry goods and seasonings: soy sauce, miso, mentsuyu, dashi, nori, furikake.
  7. Rice, in 5 kg and 10 kg bags, chosen by region, variety and harvest year.
  8. Bread, snacks, drinks, frozen food, then the tills.

When the discount stickers go on — the most useful thing in this article

As closing time approaches, prepared food, lunchboxes, sashimi and meat get discount stickers: 20 per cent off, then 30, then half price.

Part of that is the shop protecting its margin. But it is also, explicitly, about not throwing food away. Food loss — food still edible when it's binned — has been a public issue here for years. The Ministry of Agriculture puts business-sector food loss at 2.37 million tonnes in the 2024 financial year; that's 57 per cent below the figure for 2000, and still that much.

The emblem of the problem was ehōmaki, the thick sushi roll eaten at the start of February. Shops piled them high, the unsold ones were thrown out that night, and the photographs ran in the news every year until the criticism stuck. The Ministry of Agriculture now recruits retailers who commit to cutting ehōmaki waste and gives them a logo to display — the government naming a seasonal product's leftovers as a problem.

Supermarkets have been getting inventive about it too. One chain brought in a system that uses AI to decide when to mark things down (March 2026). A chain in Kyoto had high-school students design friendlier discount stickers and reported unsold stock halving in trials (April 2026). Another turned buying reduced items into a game with prizes (August 2026). Over at the convenience stores, FamilyMart released a "crying face" discount sticker design for anyone to use (November 2025). The sticker on that lunchbox at seven in the evening sits at the end of all that. Buying it does a small amount of work.

Understand what you're buying Discounted food is food near its date. Japan uses two of them, and the difference matters. 消費期限 (shōhi kigen) is a use-by date for safety — it goes on lunchboxes, prepared food and sashimi, and you don't eat past it. 賞味期限 (shōmi kigen) is a best-before for quality, on tinned food, snacks and seasonings; passing it doesn't make the food unsafe. Most stickered items are close to the first kind, so the assumption is that you eat them today. If you want maximum freshness, if your stomach is unreliable, or if you're feeding a small child or an elderly relative, buy from the full-price shelf instead.

Two practical notes:

  • Waiting for half price often means the thing you wanted has gone. Buying at 30 per cent off is the realistic move.
  • Discounted sashimi assumes you eat it today. Don't carry it back to the hotel for tomorrow's breakfast.

What's actually worth buying

Light, hard to get elsewhere, and genuinely used here.

  • Japanese seasonings — the most interesting category by far. Worcester-style sauce comes in regional versions; so does mayonnaise. Put two brands side by side and they don't taste the same.
  • Furikake — dried seasoning to shake over rice. Dozens of kinds, and it weighs nothing.
  • Instant miso soup (see the miso soup article).
  • Mentsuyu — a small bottle travels.
  • Tea bags — barley tea, roasted green tea.
  • Curry roux — the block kind, in mild to hot.
  • Snacks. The range is enormous, there are regional editions, and supermarket prices beat the convenience store on most of them.

The till, step by step

  1. Take a basket from the stack by the door.
  2. Queue. Most supermarkets now also have self-checkouts, and they're widespread enough that you can expect to find them.
  3. Bags cost money — a few yen. Most people carry their own.
  4. Pay with cash, card, IC card or a QR app. All of them generally work.
  5. The packing bench. Past the till there's a long counter where you bag your own shopping. This is the step that catches people out: the cashier doesn't pack for you.
  6. You'll be asked about a points card. "No" is a complete answer.

Reading a price label

  • Tax. Both the tax-excluded and tax-included prices are usually shown, and the bigger number is normally the one without tax — it looks cheaper that way. Check before you're surprised at the till.
  • Per 100 g for meat and fish; what the pack costs depends on its weight.
  • The growing region (◯◯県産). Japanese shoppers look at this first.
  • Harvest year on rice: 令和8年産 means the 2026 crop, this year's new rice.
  • Use-by versus best-before, as above — the sticker usually means the first one is near.

Right now: what's happening in the aisles (September 2026)

  • Rice has come down. The supermarket average was a little over ¥3,100 for 5 kg in early September (NHK), and this year's new crop is undercutting last year's stock — see the rice article.
  • Eggs are still high, around ¥305 for ten in early August 2026, about 20 per cent above normal.
  • Tax-free shopping changes on 1 November 2026. Purchases from that date move to a refund system: you pay the full tax-included price in the shop, and the consumption tax is refunded after customs confirms at the airport or port that the goods are leaving the country. It applies to purchases of ¥5,000 or more before tax, in one shop, on one day. Only some supermarkets are tax-free registered, so this is about shops displaying the sign. The Japan Tourism Agency publishes an English leaflet. How the refund reaches you differs by shop and operator, so ask at the till.
  • Prices are still rising. Teikoku Databank counted 4,923 food items going up in September 2026, seasonings the largest group at 1,959.
